# A new device gets in by its sign-in: what's left

Andre's decision, 2026-11-05, built that day but for what's left below. A sign-in the person's own devices can check without trusting auth (an ID token or a passkey's assertion naming the new device and the network) lets one of them seal the new device its keys with no tap; any other asks with one tap (ARCHITECTURE.md's Trust). The code is `src/sign_in_proof.rs` (the checks), `cloudflare/auth/worker.js` (the nonce, the challenge, passing the proof on, telling a sign-in from a link, and the pushes), `src/node/devices.rs` and `cloudflare/web/web.js` (approving by it), and Settings › Devices (joined).

Left:
- Push: a silent push that wakes the phone to check. Auth pushes "wants in" (with the action `{ "join": "<code>" }`, which the apps show with Approve and Not Me: `client/native/iphone/Notifications/Notes.swift`, `android/…/Push.kt`, `client/native/windows/Notes.cs`) when nothing let a device in within 10 seconds, and "joined" once one is; a phone woken by "wants in" approves only through its normal approval path.
- Auth knows whom to push to as the devices that asked to join as that person, browsers that say they're theirs as they look for devices waiting, and every device whose push registration (signed by its key) names its person, as each app and browser does when it registers from 2026-11-06's build (the Apple apps at each launch). An app on an older build is known only once it takes that build. Not yet seen on a real phone: APNs, FCM and WNS wait on the Apple, Firebase and Entra accounts.
- A passkey's public key is recorded from what auth holds when a device that signed in with it is approved by its code, so a tampered auth then could slip in its own: as with a tampered auth deploy, "joined · Remove" is what shows it.

Considered and declined (Andre, 11-05): letting a new device in with none of the person's devices online, by a Worker that holds keys or by a secret kept with the provider (a Drive app folder). Approval stays with the person's own clients.

## What it trusts

- The provider, not auth: whoever controls the person's Google (or Apple, Microsoft) account, or their passkey, can add a device. Their 2-step verification matters.
- Auth runs the sign-in but can't make the proof: a bug in auth, or someone reading its database, lets no device in by itself. What a device is checked against is only what the person's own devices recorded of their own clients (an admin's device recording a machine or someone's first device counts for nothing), and an ID token counts only for the network's own client, named by the `sign_in_clients` setting an admin's device signs as it deploys to Cloudflare, never by auth. A proof names the network (its root) as well as the device.
- Where auth is still trusted: when a person approves a device by its code, what that device signed in with is recorded. For Google that's checked against Google's keys and the network's client, so a tampered auth can't plant its own account. For a passkey, the public key comes from auth, which keeps it: passkeys are made on auth's own page, so no client sees one made without auth's code in between. The approval card names what's recorded ("a@example.com · Google", or "Passkey"), and each device's ⋯ › Sign-ins… shows it and removes it, by a step every device of theirs honours.
- Not Me sticks: it's a step that takes the device's key out before it's ever listed, so none of the person's devices lets it in, by its sign-in or its code, and auth forgets its proof. A browser refuses to seal anything to a key its own copy of who's who has taken out, whatever a node says.
- Link phishing (Andre, 11-05): someone could start a sign-in on their own machine (the CLI's `sign-in`), send the person its link, and a Google pick or passkey tap would sign in their device. So a device is let in by its sign-in only when it signed in itself, in its own browser or our apps' own sign-in on it. Auth tells which by the client whose token asks to join: if its every sign-in comes back to the device it was made on (the web client's page, `<cli>://signed-in/`, loopback), it did; if it comes back to the sign-in code's page or a site, it was finished from a link, and auth passes the proof on as `{ "by_link": … }`. A device of theirs then records what it signed in with only when the person taps Approve on its card, which names the device and where it asked from; it never lets it in by itself. Under an honest auth phishing can't change where a sign-in comes back to; a tampered auth could mislabel one, which is the Cloudflare account's trust as below. The CLI always signs in by its link, so it always waits for a tap.
- A tampered auth deploy could still get a person to sign in for its own device, as the browsers' code it serves could already take what a browser opens; that's the Cloudflare account's trust, and the "joined · Remove" notice is what shows it.
- An older device (a phone or Mac behind) doesn't know Not Me's or Sign-ins' steps and goes on as before; its check fails on a sign-in naming the network, so it lets that device in only by code. It reads a `by_link` sign-in as none, so it too lets that device in only by code.
